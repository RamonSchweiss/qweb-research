# Does a quantum claim cause the classical improvement? Report

**Date:** 8 November 2026.
**Governed by:** `designs/design_moving_baseline_causality_EN.md`, version 1,
registered the same day before any searching.

**Outcome: two STATED, one weaker.** By section 6, the claim stands as I made
it, and the article may use the causal form with quotations as its evidence.

**But it was not supported when I made it.** The comment went out this morning;
the evidence was gathered this afternoon. The claim turned out to be true, which
is not the same as having been justified. That distinction is the reason this
note exists and it belongs in the record.

---

## A. Dequantisation of recommendation systems — **STATED**

Ewin Tang, "A quantum-inspired classical algorithm for recommendation systems",
arXiv 1807.04271. The introduction states the motivation directly:

> "This work stems from failed efforts to prove that Kerenidis and Prakash's
> quantum recommendation [algorithm gives an exponential speedup]"

and the purpose:

> "The goal of this work is to describe a classical algorithm that performs the
> same task … with only polynomially slower runtime, thus answering this
> question."

This is the strongest possible form of the causal link, and **stronger than the
version I posted this morning.** The work did not begin as an attempt to beat
the quantum algorithm. It began as an attempt to **prove the quantum speedup
real**, and the classical algorithm is what the failure of that attempt
produced. The quantum claim did not merely provoke a competitor; it set the
problem that destroyed it.

## B. Classical simulation of the Sycamore sampling claim — **STATED**

Pan and Zhang, "Solving the Sampling Problem of the Sycamore Quantum Circuits",
arXiv 2111.03011. The abstract names the target:

> "We study the problem of generating independent samples from the output
> distribution of Google's Sycamore quantum circuits"

and the introduction gives the reason the work exists:

> "the computational time estimated by Google relies on a specific classical
> algorithm"

> "there could exist algorithms that perform much better than the algorithm used
> by Google"

The quantum result supplies the problem instance, the benchmark and the reason.
Under section 1 that is STATED rather than merely implied: the paper says why it
was undertaken, not only what it cites.

## C. Annealing advantage claims against classical heuristics — **weaker than
IMPLIED, and this is the interesting one**

Rønnow et al., "Defining and detecting quantum speedup". The abstract and
introduction motivate the work with the general question of how to assess
speedup as small devices appear. The D-Wave Two comparison is presented as a
test case, and neither passage ties the work to a specific advantage claim.

So the case most often *told* as "the classical side caught up" has the
weakest documentary link of the three. The methodology is general; the device is
an instance of it. Calling it a response would be reconstructing a motive from
the shape of the work, which section 4 names as the invalidating condition.

**Recorded as not supporting the claim.** It is not evidence against it either —
only that this case does not carry it.

## D. A limitation in how C was read, stated because it cuts against me

Cases A and B are supported by **verbatim quotations**. Case C rests on a
report that the abstract and introduction contain no such statement — an
absence, reported by a summarising fetch rather than read directly.

An absence is weaker evidence than a quotation, and a summariser can miss a
sentence it was not looking for. The classification of C should therefore be
treated as provisional in a way that A and B need not be. If C matters to a
later argument, the paper should be read directly.

## E. What this does and does not establish

**Does:** that in two documented cases, the authors of the classical work state
that the quantum result is why the work was undertaken. The causal claim is not
a just-so story in those two.

**Does not:** that this is common. Three named cases cannot measure prevalence
and this report is not written as though they could. Nor does it establish the
reverse direction — whether the classical improvements would have come anyway,
later, without the quantum prompt. Nothing here tests that.

## F. Consequence for the article and for the posted comment

**The article can use the causal form**, and should lead with Tang's case in its
accurate shape: an attempt to confirm a quantum speedup, which produced its
refutation. That is a better and more surprising story than "the classical side
responded", and it is in the author's own words.

**The posted comment needs no correction on its facts.** It needs one on its
footing, and only if the subject comes up: the causal half was asserted before
it was checked, and happened to hold. An unverified claim that turns out true is
a different object from a verified one, and this programme has spent a week on
exactly that distinction.

**And case C should be the article's own counterweight.** The example everyone
reaches for first is the one whose paper trail does not support the story. An
article arguing that comparisons age badly ought to say which of its own
examples is the weakest.
